Replication and broker failure

Servers die. Disks fail. Kafka's answer is to keep several copies of every partition on different brokers and to switch to a copy automatically. This module shows exactly how, and lets you break a cluster on purpose.

1. The idea: keep copies

The replication factor (RF) says how many copies of each partition exist. With RF=3 every partition lives on 3 different brokers. Your lab topics use RF=3.

AnalogyOne person keeps the master notebook (the leader) and everyone writes in it through them. Two assistants (the followers) keep photocopies and update them every few seconds. If the master person falls ill, an assistant with an up-to-date copy takes over, and nothing is lost.
Leader

One replica per partition. All reads and writes go here.

Follower

The other replicas. They only copy from the leader. They serve no client traffic by default.

ISR

In-Sync Replicas: the leader plus followers that are fully caught up. Only ISR members can become leader.

3. Break it yourself: the cluster simulator

This is a working model of your lab cluster: 3 brokers, a topic with 3 partitions, leaders spread out as in your real cluster. Change the settings, send messages, kill and start brokers, and watch the event log explain what Kafka decides.

Simulator: 3-broker Kafka cluster
Replication factor min.insync.replicas acks
Producer idle.
Experiments (do them in order)
  1. Survive one failure. Defaults (RF 3, min 2, acks all). Send a few messages. Kill broker 0. Send more: still works. Look at Isr shrink and the leader move. Start broker 0 and watch it catch up and rejoin.
  2. Survive two failures, but refuse writes. Kill two brokers. Writes now fail with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS because only 1 in-sync replica is left and min is 2. Nothing is lost; Kafka protects you from a write that would sit on a single disk.
  3. Choose availability over safety. Set min.insync.replicas to 1, kill two brokers, and send: it works, but now every message lives on one broker only.
  4. Lose data on purpose. Set acks=1 and press the Demo button. The message is acknowledged, the leader dies before copying it, and the log reports it lost. Repeat with acks=all.
  5. No redundancy. Set RF=1 and kill a broker: partitions on it go offline. That is what "no replication" means.

4. Read the real thing

The simulator's grey panel copies the layout of the real kafka-topics.sh --describe output. Here is what your actual cluster printed when we killed kafka-2 during testing:

Real output from your cluster 
Partition: 0  Leader: 1  Replicas: 1,2,0  Isr: 1,0      # broker 2 dropped out of the ISR
Partition: 1  Leader: 0  Replicas: 2,0,1  Isr: 0,1      # leader moved 2 -> 0 automatically
Partition: 2  Leader: 0  Replicas: 0,1,2  Isr: 0,1
... a few seconds after the pod restarted ...
Partition: 0  Leader: 1  Replicas: 1,2,0  Isr: 1,0,2    # broker 2 caught up and rejoined
  • Replicas = where copies live (the first is the "preferred" leader).
  • Isr = the copies that are in sync right now. Healthy means Isr equals Replicas.
  • Producing with acks=all kept working during the outage, because 2 in-sync replicas is still at least min.insync.replicas=2.

And when we took two of three brokers away, the producer reported NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS, the write was refused, and after both returned all earlier data was intact. That is the safety net doing its job.

6. How a write is committed

Followers copy from the leader a little behind it. Kafka calls a record committed only when every replica in the ISR has it. The highest committed position is the high watermark. Consumers can only read up to the high watermark, so they never see data that could vanish if the leader died.

7. Leader election, and the dangerous option

When a leader dies, the controller picks a new one from the ISR, which guarantees the new leader has every committed record. If the ISR has nobody alive, the partition goes offline and clients get LEADER_NOT_AVAILABLE.

Unclean leader electionThe setting unclean.leader.election.enable (default false) would let an out-of-sync replica take over to restore availability. It brings the partition back sooner but permanently loses the records that replica was missing. Leave it off for important data.

8. How this maps to your Kubernetes cluster

  • Anti-affinity: the StatefulSet forces each broker pod onto a different worker node, so a node failure costs at most one replica.
  • PodDisruptionBudget (minAvailable 2): a kubectl drain cannot take a second broker while the first is still recovering.
  • Persistent volumes: a restarted broker keeps its data and only has to catch up on what it missed.
  • Controller quorum: your three brokers are also the three KRaft controllers, so you need 2 of 3 nodes alive for cluster metadata operations (module 6).
  • For real clusters you would also set broker.rack to spread replicas across availability zones.
